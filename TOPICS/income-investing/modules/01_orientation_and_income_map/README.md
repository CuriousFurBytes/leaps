## Theory

### The Core Mental Model

Orientation and Income Map begins with a simple distinction: income is not one thing. It can be earned directly through labor, produced by a business system, generated by an owned asset, or paid by a financial investment. Each category has different risks, time requirements, tax treatment, and uncertainty. Educational planning means describing those differences before choosing an opportunity.

Historically, many households relied on one dominant wage source. Platform work, online marketplaces, low-cost software tools, and accessible brokerage platforms changed the menu of possible income experiments. That expansion is useful, but it also increased noise. A learner needs a filter that asks: Who pays? Why do they pay? What costs are hidden? What risks remain after the first payment?

```python
# Educational example: classify income streams by labor intensity.
# This is a learning model, not a recommendation to pursue any stream.
streams = [
    {"name": "freelance editing", "hours": 8, "net_cash": 320},
    {"name": "template download", "hours": 2, "net_cash": 60},
]
for stream in streams:
    net_hourly = stream["net_cash"] / stream["hours"]
    print(stream["name"], f"${net_hourly:.2f} per hour")
```

### Unit Economics

Unit economics translate an idea into a small accounting model. For a gig, the unit might be one delivery block, one tutoring session, one design package, or one digital sale. The learner calculates revenue, direct costs, platform fees, travel, preparation time, support time, and reserves for taxes or refunds. The result is not a perfect forecast; it is a discipline for seeing tradeoffs.

```csv

One tutoring session,60,5,0,20
One marketplace sale,35,8,4,15
One local delivery block,72,18,0,30
```

### Risk, Records, and Decision Rules

The safest educational habit is to write decision rules before excitement takes over. Examples include: do not buy inventory until a pilot sells, do not count gross revenue as profit, do not call income passive until maintenance is measured, and do not act on investment claims you cannot explain. Records turn vague feelings into evidence.

```python
# Educational example: compare gross revenue with net income.
gross_revenue = 1000
costs = 260
estimated_reserve = 200
net_after_reserve = gross_revenue - costs - estimated_reserve
print(f"Spendable estimate: ${net_after_reserve}")
```

## Key Concepts

- **Gross revenue:** Money received before costs. It can look impressive while hiding low profit.
- **Net income:** Money left after expenses and reserves. This is more useful for comparing opportunities.
- **Opportunity cost:** The value of the next-best use of time or money. It keeps side projects honest.
- **Scalability:** The degree to which revenue can grow without equal growth in labor. It is never unlimited.
- **Risk control:** A preplanned boundary that limits damage from mistakes, fraud, taxes, or volatility.

## Common Pitfalls

### Mistake: Counting revenue as profit

```python
# Wrong: treats every dollar received as spendable.
revenue = 500
spendable = revenue
print(spendable)
```

```python
# Better: subtract costs and reserves first.
revenue = 500
costs = 120
reserve = 100
spendable = revenue - costs - reserve
print(spendable)
```

### Mistake: Ignoring unpaid time

```csv
wrong_field,value
paid_hours,5
admin_hours,0
```

```csv
better_field,value
paid_hours,5
admin_hours,2
```

Ignoring admin time overstates hourly return and makes weak ideas look strong.

### Mistake: Confusing passive with effortless

```markdown
Wrong: "This product sold once, so it is passive forever."
```

```markdown
Better: "This product has lower delivery labor, but still needs updates, support, taxes, and marketing."
```
