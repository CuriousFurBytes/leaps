## Overview

Module 01 gave you the building blocks of French pronunciation and a handful of essential phrases. Module 02 expands those into functional communication: the ability to introduce yourself, ask basic questions, understand and give numbers up to 100, say the date, and tell time — all while navigating the formal/informal register distinction that governs French social interaction.

This module also introduces your first French question structures and your first glimpse of the verb *être* (to be) in context — not as a grammar topic (that is Module 04) but as a functional tool: *Je suis de Paris.* (I am from Paris.) *Il est étudiant.* (He is a student.) These constructions will be formalized in Module 04; here, they are learned as functional patterns.

By the end of Module 02, you will be able to hold a short, self-contained conversation with a French speaker covering introductions, origins, and basic information exchange. This is your first real communicative milestone in French.

## Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

1. Introduce yourself and others in French, providing name, nationality, and profession
2. Ask and answer basic questions using *Qui? Quoi? Où? Quand? Comment? Combien?*
3. Count and use numbers from 1 to 100, including the irregular 70s and 90s
4. Say the days of the week and months of the year correctly
5. Tell the time in French using both formal (24-hour) and informal (12-hour) expressions
6. State and ask for dates: day, month, year
7. Use polite filler phrases, conversation openers, and responses (*Vraiment ? C'est vrai. Je ne sais pas. D'accord.*)
8. Distinguish formal and informal register in vocabulary and speech acts beyond *tu/vous*

## Theory

### Introducing Yourself in French

The core pattern for introducing yourself is:
```text
Je m'appelle [name].          I am called [name]. / My name is [name].
Je suis [nationality/job].    I am [nationality/job].
J'habite à [city].            I live in [city].
Je suis de [city/country].    I am from [city/country].
```

**Introducing others:**
```text
Voici [name]. C'est mon ami(e) [name].
Je vous présente [name].   (formal)
Je te présente [name].     (informal)
```

**Asking for information:**
```text
Comment vous appelez-vous ?   What is your name? (formal)
Comment tu t'appelles ?       What is your name? (informal)
Vous êtes de quelle ville ?   What city are you from? (formal)
D'où tu viens ?               Where are you from? (informal)
```

**Nationalities** (note: they are lowercase in French, unlike English):

| Nationality | Masculine | Feminine |
|------------|-----------|----------|
| French | *français* | *française* |
| English | *anglais* | *anglaise* |
| American | *américain* | *américaine* |
| Spanish | *espagnol* | *espagnole* |
| German | *allemand* | *allemande* |
| Italian | *italien* | *italienne* |
| Brazilian | *brésilien* | *brésilienne* |
| Canadian | *canadien* | *canadienne* |
| Japanese | *japonais* | *japonaise* |
| Chinese | *chinois* | *chinoise* |

> [!NOTE]
> In French, nationalities used as adjectives (after *être*) are lowercase: *Je suis français.* But when used as a noun (referring to a person), they are uppercase: *Un Français* (a Frenchman). This rule applies consistently and is one of the clearest differences from English capitalization.

### Numbers 21–100

Building on numbers 1–20, French numbers follow predictable patterns — with notable exceptions in the 70s and 90s that require special attention.

**21–69: Regular pattern**

The pattern from 21–69 is: tens number + *et* (for 1s) or tens + unit number (for 2–9).

```text
21 = vingt et un      (vingt + et + un)
22 = vingt-deux       (vingt + deux)
30 = trente
31 = trente et un
40 = quarante
50 = cinquante
60 = soixante
61 = soixante et un
69 = soixante-neuf
```

**70–79: The infamous vigesimal system**

French does NOT have a word for "seventy." Instead:
```text
70 = soixante-dix         (sixty-ten)
71 = soixante et onze     (sixty-eleven)
72 = soixante-douze       (sixty-twelve)
73 = soixante-treize
...
79 = soixante-dix-neuf
```

This is a legacy of a vigesimal (base-20) counting system inherited from a Celtic substrate. Belgium and Switzerland use *septante* (70) instead — much simpler, but non-standard in France.

**80–89: Also vigesimal**

```text
80 = quatre-vingts        (four-twenties)    Note: the s on vingts!
81 = quatre-vingt-un      (four-twenty-one)  No s when followed by a number
82 = quatre-vingt-deux
...
89 = quatre-vingt-neuf
```

Note: *quatre-vingts* (80) has a final *s* when standing alone, but loses it when followed by another number: *quatre-vingt-deux*.

**90–99: Doubly unusual**

```text
90 = quatre-vingt-dix      (four-twenty-ten)
91 = quatre-vingt-onze
...
99 = quatre-vingt-dix-neuf
```

**100:**
```text
100 = cent         /sɑ̃/
101 = cent un      (no et, unlike 21, 31, etc.)
200 = deux cents   (the s appears on cent when there is no following number)
201 = deux cent un (s disappears)
```

### Days of the Week (les jours de la semaine)

Days in French are not capitalized (unless at the start of a sentence).

| French | Pronunciation | Day |
|--------|--------------|-----|
| *lundi* | /lœ̃.di/ | Monday |
| *mardi* | /maʁ.di/ | Tuesday |
| *mercredi* | /mɛʁ.kʁə.di/ | Wednesday |
| *jeudi* | /ʒø.di/ | Thursday |
| *vendredi* | /vɑ̃.dʁə.di/ | Friday |
| *samedi* | /sam.di/ | Saturday |
| *dimanche* | /di.mɑ̃ʃ/ | Sunday |

Note: The French week traditionally starts on **Monday** (lundi), not Sunday.

**Useful day expressions:**
```text
aujourd'hui  /o.ʒuʁ.dɥi/    today
demain       /də.mɛ̃/         tomorrow
hier         /jɛʁ/           yesterday
lundi prochain                next Monday
lundi dernier                 last Monday
le lundi (with article)       on Mondays (recurring)
```

### Months of the Year (les mois de l'année)

Also not capitalized:

| French | Pronunciation | Month |
|--------|--------------|-------|
| *janvier* | /ʒɑ̃.vje/ | January |
| *février* | /fe.vʁije/ | February |
| *mars* | /maʁs/ | March |
| *avril* | /a.vʁil/ | April |
| *mai* | /mɛ/ | May |
| *juin* | /ʒɥɛ̃/ | June |
| *juillet* | /ʒɥi.jɛ/ | July |
| *août* | /u/ or /ut/ | August |
| *septembre* | /sɛp.tɑ̃bʁ/ | September |
| *octobre* | /ɔk.tɔbʁ/ | October |
| *novembre* | /nɔ.vɑ̃bʁ/ | November |
| *décembre* | /de.sɑ̃bʁ/ | December |

**Saying the date:**
```text
On est le [number] [month].      It is the [date].
Nous sommes le [number] [month].
C'est le [number] [month].

Examples:
C'est le 3 mars.           It is March 3rd.
On est le 25 décembre.     It is December 25th.
C'est le premier janvier.  It is January 1st. (premier for 1st, not un)
```

### Telling Time (l'heure)

**Asking:**
```text
Quelle heure est-il ?         What time is it? (formal)
Il est quelle heure ?         What time is it? (informal)
Tu as l'heure ?               Do you have the time? (informal)
```

**Telling the time:**
```text
Il est [number] heure(s).

Il est une heure.             It is 1 o'clock.   (heure = singular when 1)
Il est deux heures.           It is 2 o'clock.   (heures = plural when > 1)
Il est midi.                  It is noon.
Il est minuit.                It is midnight.
Il est trois heures et demie. It is 3:30.        (et demie = and a half)
Il est quatre heures et quart. It is 4:15.       (et quart = and a quarter)
Il est cinq heures moins le quart. It is 4:45.   (moins le quart = minus a quarter)
Il est sept heures vingt.     It is 7:20.
Il est neuf heures moins cinq. It is 8:55.       (minus five)
```

**24-hour time (official schedules, transport):**
```text
Il est quatorze heures trente.  It is 14:30 (2:30pm).
Le train part à dix-neuf heures. The train leaves at 19:00.
```

### Essential Conversation Phrases

These phrases fill the gaps in conversation and make you sound more natural:

```text
Vraiment ?              Really?
C'est vrai.             That's true. / Really.
Je ne sais pas.         I don't know.
D'accord.               OK / Agreed.
Bien sûr.               Of course.
Peut-être.              Maybe.
Pas du tout.            Not at all.
Un peu.                 A little.
Je comprends.           I understand.
Je ne comprends pas.    I don't understand.
Pouvez-vous répéter ?   Can you repeat? (formal)
Tu peux répéter ?       Can you repeat? (informal)
Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît.  More slowly, please.
Comment dit-on... en français ?   How do you say... in French?
```

## Key Concepts

**Nationalities are adjectives in French.** They are not capitalized when used adjectivally after *être* (*Je suis français*), but are capitalized when used as nouns (*un Français*). They have masculine and feminine forms — learning both forms from the start avoids confusion later.

**The vigesimal counting system (base 20) for 70–99** is a genuine historical curiosity. It comes from a Celtic substrate language (Gaulish) that used base 20. Belgium, Switzerland, and the Democratic Republic of Congo use simplified alternatives (*septante, octante/huitante, nonante*), but these are not used in France. Every learner must memorize the French system.

**Days and months are lowercase** in French — this is a direct difference from English and a frequent written error for anglophones.

**Time expressions** follow a logical pattern based on subtraction and addition relative to the hour. Fractions of the hour: *et quart* (+15 min), *et demie* (+30 min), *moins le quart* (−15 min). Specific minutes: just add the number: *il est sept heures vingt* (7h20). After 30, use *moins* (minus): *il est huit heures moins dix* (7h50 — eight minus ten).

## Examples

### Example 1: A Complete Introduction

```text
Situation: First day of a French language class. The teacher asks students to introduce themselves.

Student: Bonjour, je m'appelle Sophie Chen. Je suis canadienne —
         je suis de Vancouver. J'habite maintenant à Paris.
         Je suis étudiante en architecture. J'apprends le français
         depuis six mois. Je suis ravie d'être ici !

Translation:
Hello, my name is Sophie Chen. I am Canadian — I am from Vancouver.
I now live in Paris. I am a student of architecture. I have been learning
French for six months. I am delighted to be here!

Notes:
- canadienne: feminine form of canadien (Sophie is female)
- étudiante: feminine form of étudiant (student)
- Preposition + city: je suis de [city], j'habite à [city]
- ravie: feminine form of ravi (delighted) — adjective agrees with subject
```

### Example 2: Numbers in Context

```text
Counting practice with real-world contexts:

Phone number: Mon numéro, c'est le 06 - 72 - 45 - 89 - 13.
              zéro-six, soixante-douze, quarante-cinq,
              quatre-vingt-neuf, treize

Note: French phone numbers are read in pairs of two digits.

Price: Ça coûte quatre-vingt-quinze euros et cinquante centimes.
       That costs 95 euros and 50 cents. (€95.50)

Age: J'ai vingt-trois ans.   I am 23 years old.
     Elle a soixante-dix-sept ans.  She is 77 years old.
```

### Example 3: Making an Appointment

```text
A: Pouvez-vous me voir mercredi ?
   Can you see me on Wednesday?

B: Mercredi, c'est difficile. Jeudi, ça va ?
   Wednesday is difficult. Thursday — does that work?

A: Oui, jeudi. Il est quelle heure, jeudi ?
   Yes, Thursday. What time on Thursday?

B: Dix heures et demie du matin. C'est bon pour vous ?
   10:30 in the morning. Is that OK for you?

A: Parfait. À jeudi, donc !
   Perfect. See you Thursday then!

Notes:
- du matin: in the morning (distinguishes am from pm for 12-hour clock)
- de l'après-midi: in the afternoon
- du soir: in the evening
- Donc /dɔ̃k/: therefore/then — a useful discourse connector
```

## Common Pitfalls

**Pitfall 1: Confusing 70s and 90s.**

*Soixante-dix* (70) and *quatre-vingt-dix* (90) are consistently confused with each other and with 60 and 80. Drill these until automatic. The mnemonic: "After 69, French counting *breaks*."

```text
Wrong: 70 = soixante-*quatorze*  ← treating it like a regular tens decade
       90 = *nonante*            ← this is Swiss/Belgian French, not French French

Correct: 70 = soixante-dix  (60 + 10)
         71 = soixante et onze  (60 + 11)
         90 = quatre-vingt-dix  (4×20 + 10)
         91 = quatre-vingt-onze (4×20 + 11)
```

**Pitfall 2: Capitalizing nationalities and days/months.**

English capitalizes all of these; French does not (in normal sentence use).

```text
Wrong: Je suis Français. Je parle Français tous les Lundis.
Correct: Je suis français. Je parle français tous les lundis.
```

**Pitfall 3: Using *un* instead of *premier* for the first of the month.**

For ordinal numbers in dates, French uses the cardinal number (2nd = *deux*, 15th = *quinze*), with the single exception of the 1st: *le premier*.

```text
Wrong:  C'est le un mai.
Correct: C'est le premier mai.
```

## Summary

- Self-introductions in French use *Je m'appelle, Je suis, J'habite à, Je suis de*
- Nationalities: lowercase as adjectives after être; feminine forms usually add -e; some have irregular feminines
- Numbers 21–69 follow a regular pattern; 70–79 = *soixante + 10-19*; 80–89 = *quatre-vingt + 1-9*; 90–99 = *quatre-vingt + 10-19*
- Days and months are not capitalized in French
- The first day of the month uses *premier*, not *un*
- Time: *Il est [number] heure(s)* — singular for 1 o'clock, plural otherwise; *et quart/demie*, *moins le quart* for quarter-hours
- Essential filler phrases (*D'accord, Je ne sais pas, Vraiment?*) make conversation flow naturally
