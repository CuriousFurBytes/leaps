# Answers: Module 02 — Greetings and Basic Communication

## Answer Key

### Easy Questions (1 pt each)

**Q1.** 70 = *soixante-dix*, 80 = *quatre-vingts*, 90 = *quatre-vingt-dix*, 71 = *soixante et onze*, 91 = *quatre-vingt-onze*

**Q2.** In French, days and months are common nouns, not proper nouns. The capitalization convention for proper nouns in French is narrower than in English — it applies to names of people, places, organizations, and titles, but not to the names of time units like days and months. This is a consistent rule, not an exception.

**Q3.** *Bonsoir* is a greeting used when arriving in the late afternoon or evening (roughly from 6pm onward). *Bonne soirée* is a farewell — said when leaving in the evening, wishing someone a pleasant rest of the evening. The parallel is *bonjour* (arrival greeting, day) vs. *bonne journée* (departure farewell, day). You say *bonsoir* when you walk in; you say *bonne soirée* when you walk out.

**Q4.** *C'est le premier mars.* The first day of the month uses *premier* (the ordinal adjective for "first") rather than *un* (the cardinal number "one"). All other days of the month use cardinal numbers: *le deux, le trois*, etc. Only the first of the month is exceptional.

**Q5.** *américain* → *américaine*; *espagnol* → *espagnole*; *canadien* → *canadienne* (double n); *japonais* → *japonaise*

### Medium Questions (2 pts each)

**Q6.** The French vigesimal system reflects a historical base-20 counting system from a Celtic substrate (Gaulish) that predates the Roman conquest. From 70–99, rather than using tens words (septante, octante, nonante), standard French builds on 60 and 80: 70 = soixante-dix (60+10), 71 = soixante et onze (60+11), 80 = quatre-vingts (4×20), 90 = quatre-vingt-dix (4×20+10). Belgium, Switzerland, and some African francophone countries use the more logical *septante* (70), *huitante* or *octante* (80), *nonante* (90) — but these are non-standard in France. France's Académie Française has declined to adopt them despite their logical simplicity.

**Q7.** Sample answer: *Bonjour, je m'appelle Claire Petit. Je suis française — je suis de Bordeaux, dans le sud-ouest de la France. Je suis comptable. Je suis ravie de vous rencontrer.*

**Q8.** French time uses *Il est* + hour + minutes. For "o'clock": *Il est [number] heure(s)*. For minutes past: state the hour then the minutes (*Il est sept heures vingt* = 7:20). For 30 minutes: *et demie* (*Il est trois heures et demie* = 3:30). For 15 minutes: *et quart* (*Il est neuf heures et quart* = 9:15). For minutes before the next hour: use *moins* (*Il est huit heures moins dix* = 7:50 — 8 minus 10 minutes). For 45 minutes: *moins le quart* (*Il est six heures moins le quart* = 5:45).

**Q9.** Nationalities used as **adjectives** (after a verb like *être*) are lowercase: *Je suis française* (I am French), *Il est canadien* (He is Canadian). Nationalities used as **nouns** (referring to a person as a member of a national group) are capitalized: *une Française* (a French woman), *les Canadiens* (the Canadians). When an adjective, no capital; when a noun, capital.

**Q10.** *Salut ! Il est quelle heure ? — Presque neuf heures. T'as cours ? — Oui, à neuf heures et demie. À tout à l'heure !*

### Hard Questions (3 pts each)

**Q11.** Phone number: 06 93 79 81 52. Availability: Thursday between 2:00pm and 4:30pm.

**Q12.** Acceptable answer: a dialogue using *vous*, formal greetings and farewells, correct names/origins/destinations, at least 3 annotations explaining choices.

**Q13.** Errors: (1) *Français* should be *français* — adjective after être, lowercase; (2) *Américain* should be *américain* — same rule; (3) *Français* (second occurrence, adjective in *parlons Français*) should be *français*; (4) *Mardis* should be *mardis* — days of the week are not capitalized. Corrected: *Je suis français et mon ami est américain. Nous parlons français ensemble tous les mardis.*

**Q14.** Accept any 3 of the following types of situations with reasonable explanations: meeting an employer/interviewer, addressing a professor, speaking to an older stranger, contacting a business/service, official contexts (doctors, officials, bank staff), first meeting at a professional event. The consequence of inappropriate *tu* is perceived rudeness, arrogance, or at minimum a breach of social convention that creates awkwardness.

### Expert Questions (5 pts each)

**Q15.** Strategies and techniques: Accept well-reasoned answers covering chunking (reading 79 as soixante + dix-neuf rather than processing each digit), frequent drilling (especially 71–79 and 91–99), comparing with Belgian/Swiss forms to understand the structure, using real-world contexts (prices, ages, phone numbers) for immersive practice.

**Q16.** Accept answers that compare the French *tu/vous* system with German (*du/Sie*), Spanish (*tú/usted*), Italian (*tu/Lei*), or other relevant languages. Strong answers note that 20th-century social changes (student revolution of May 1968, workplace democratization) accelerated tutoiement between colleagues and peers in France, and that the current situation is generationally variable — younger speakers use *tu* more broadly.
