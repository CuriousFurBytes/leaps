## Overview

Every French noun has a grammatical gender — masculine or feminine — that affects every article, adjective, and pronoun associated with it. This module explains the gender system in depth, gives you the most reliable patterns for predicting gender from spelling endings, and teaches you the three article categories (definite *le/la/les*, indefinite *un/une/des*, and partitive *du/de la/des*) that French uses where English simply uses "the" or "a" or nothing.

The partitive article is the most distinctly French feature of this module: it appears in constructions like *Je mange du pain* (I eat bread/some bread) and *Je bois de l'eau* (I drink water), where English uses a bare noun. Getting the article system right is fundamental to grammatical fluency.

## Theory

### Grammatical Gender: What It Is and Why It Matters

In French, every noun belongs to one of two grammatical categories: **masculin** or **féminin**. This is a property of the word, not a reflection of physical sex or biological reality. *Un stylo* (a pen) is masculine; *une table* (a table) is feminine. *Le courage* (courage) is masculine; *la liberté* (freedom) is feminine.

Gender matters because it controls **agreement** (accord) throughout the sentence. Articles, adjectives, pronouns, and past participles must match the gender of the noun they accompany. Getting the gender wrong creates errors that cascade through the entire sentence.

**The hard truth:** There is no visual rule that applies to every French noun. A student must learn the gender of each noun, ideally from the moment of first encounter. The most reliable strategy is to learn every noun with its article: not *maison* but *la maison*; not *livre* but *le livre*. Treat the article as part of the vocabulary item.

**Common word endings that predict masculine gender:**

| Ending | Examples | Exceptions to watch |
|--------|---------|---------------------|

| -eau | le bureau, le chapeau | l'eau (fem.) |
| -isme | le capitalisme, le tourisme | |
| -ard | le canard, le regard | |
| -phone, -graphe | le téléphone, le paragraphe | |

| Ending | Examples | Exceptions |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| -tion, -sion | la nation, la décision | |

**Nouns with fixed gender regardless of person:**

Some professional/role nouns are grammatically fixed regardless of the person: *une victime* is always feminine even when referring to a male victim; *une personne* is always feminine; *un auteur* was traditionally masculine only (the Académie Française now accepts *une autrice*). This area is evolving rapidly in contemporary French due to feminist language reform advocacy.

### The Three Article Types

French uses three article systems where English uses mainly one.

**Definite articles (*le, la, l', les*) — "the"**

Used when the noun is specific, known, or unique:

```text
le livre    (the book — masculine singular)
la maison   (the house — feminine singular)
l'ami       (the friend — either gender, starts with vowel → elision)
les livres  (the books — plural, any gender)
```

Also used (unlike English) for:
- General categories: *J'aime le chocolat.* (I like chocolate — the whole category)
- Countries: *La France est belle.* (France is beautiful)
- Languages: *Je parle le français.* (I speak French)
- Days of the week in recurring sense: *Le lundi, je travaille.* (On Mondays, I work)

**Indefinite articles (*un, une, des*) — "a/an" or "some"**

Used for non-specific or introduced items:

```text
un livre    (a book — masculine singular)
une maison  (a house — feminine singular)
des livres  (some books / books — plural)
```

**Partitive articles (*du, de la, de l', des*) — "some" or no article**

Used for uncountable nouns — portions of a larger mass or abstract qualities. English uses a bare noun or "some"; French requires the partitive article:

```text
du pain         (some bread / bread — du = de + le)
de la viande    (some meat / meat — feminine)
de l'eau        (some water / water — before vowel)
des épinards    (some spinach — plural)
```

```text
Examples in context:
Je mange du pain.           I eat bread. / I'm eating (some) bread.
Il boit de l'eau.           He drinks water.
Elle a de la patience.      She has patience.
```

**The negation rule for articles:**

After *ne...pas* (and other negations), all indefinite and partitive articles become *de* (or *d'* before a vowel). The definite article does NOT change.

```text
J'ai un chien.         → Je n'ai pas de chien.        (indefinite → de)
Je mange du pain.      → Je ne mange pas de pain.     (partitive → de)
Il boit de l'eau.      → Il ne boit pas d'eau.        (partitive → d')
J'aime le café.        → Je n'aime pas le café.       (definite stays le!)
```

### Plural Formation

**Regular plurals:** Add *-s* to the singular. (The *-s* is silent in speech but triggers liaison with the next vowel-initial word.)

```text
un livre → des livres
la table → les tables
```

**Nouns ending in -s, -x, -z already:** No change in plural.

```text
un bras → des bras     (arm)
une voix → des voix    (voice)
un nez → des nez       (nose)
```

**Nouns ending in -eau, -au, -eu:** Add *-x* (not *-s*).

```text
un bureau → des bureaux    (desk/office)
un jeu → des jeux          (game)
un chapeau → des chapeaux  (hat)
Exception: un pneu → des pneus (tire) — takes -s
```

**Nouns ending in -al:** Change to *-aux*.

```text
un cheval → des chevaux    (horse)
un journal → des journaux  (newspaper)
un animal → des animaux    (animal)
Exceptions: un bal → des bals, un festival → des festivals
```

**Nouns ending in -ou:** Usually add *-s*, but 7 exceptional nouns add *-x*: *bijou, chou, genou, hibou, joujou, pou, caillou* → *bijoux, choux, genoux*, etc.

## Key Concepts

**Every noun has a gender — learn it with the article.** The single most important habit in French vocabulary learning is to memorize each noun with its article (or its grammatical gender marker). Write *la maison*, not just *maison*. This habit prevents gender errors throughout your French learning.

**The partitive article is unique to French (and similar Romance languages).** There is no equivalent in English. *Je mange du pain* literally means "I eat of-the bread" — a portion of the mass of bread. English speakers consistently forget the partitive or replace it with the definite article (*Je mange le pain* — wrong; this would mean "I eat THE bread" = a specific, identified loaf).

**After negation, articles change.** This rule affects indefinite and partitive articles only; definite articles are unaffected. *Je n'ai pas de pain* — not *de le pain*, just *de*.

**Gender patterns from endings are probabilistic, not absolute.** Knowing that *-tion* endings are almost always feminine (*la nation, la révolution*) will be right 95%+ of the time, but there are exceptions. Patterns reduce the memorization burden — they do not eliminate it.

## Examples

### Example 1: Articles with the Same Noun in Different Contexts

```text
Le pain est délicieux.          The bread is delicious. (specific loaf)
C'est un pain aux noix.         It's a walnut bread. (one type/loaf)
Je mange du pain le matin.      I eat bread in the morning. (partitive — some bread)
Je ne mange pas de pain.        I don't eat bread. (negation — de replaces du)
```

### Example 2: Plural Formation Practice

```text
Singular → Plural:
un chapeau → des chapeaux     (regular -eau → -eaux)
un journal → des journaux     (regular -al → -aux)
une voix   → des voix         (no change, already ends in x)
un bal     → des bals         (exception: bal takes -s not -aux)
```

### Example 3: Gender Prediction from Endings

```text
Nation → la nation (feminine: -tion ending)
Gouvernement → le gouvernement (masculine: -ment ending)
Beauté → la beauté (feminine: -té ending)
Voyage → le voyage (masculine: -age ending)
Fleur → la fleur (feminine exception: most -eur nouns are masculine, but fleur/chaleur/couleur/peur/soeur are feminine)
```

## Common Pitfalls

**Pitfall 1: Using the definite article for general statements in English style.**

```text
Wrong:   J'achète le pain tous les jours.   (= I buy THE bread every day — a specific, known loaf)
Correct: J'achète du pain tous les jours.   (= I buy bread every day — unspecified quantity/type)

Wrong:   Je mange la viande.               (I eat THE meat — which specific meat?)
Correct: Je mange de la viande.            (I eat meat — in general)
```

**Pitfall 2: Forgetting to change articles after negation.**

```text
Wrong:   Je n'ai pas du pain. / Je n'ai pas un stylo.
Correct: Je n'ai pas de pain. / Je n'ai pas de stylo.
```

**Pitfall 3: Making masculine nouns plural with -aux when they don't take it.**

Not every masculine noun ends in -al and takes -aux. Only nouns actually ending in *-al* make this change (and even then there are exceptions like *bal → bals*).

## Summary

- Every French noun has a grammatical gender (masculine or feminine) — learn each noun with its article
- Gender cannot be reliably determined from the thing's nature; it must be learned per word, supported by ending patterns
- Three article types: definite (*le/la/l'/les*), indefinite (*un/une/des*), partitive (*du/de la/de l'/des*)
- The partitive article is used for portions of uncountable nouns; English uses a bare noun where French uses the partitive
- After negation (ne...pas), indefinite and partitive articles become *de/d'*; definite articles do not change
- Regular plurals add *-s*; -eau/-au/-eu nouns add *-x*; -al nouns change to *-aux* (with exceptions)
