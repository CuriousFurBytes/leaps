## Overview

French belongs to the Romance branch of the Indo-European language family, descended from the Vulgar Latin spoken by Roman soldiers and settlers in Gaul (modern France) from the 1st century BCE onward. It shares this ancestry with Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, and Romanian, which means that intermediate learners often find these languages accessible once French is established. Modern Standard French — the variety taught in this curriculum — is based on the educated speech of Paris and has been codified and maintained by the Académie Française since 1635.

Today, French is an official language in 29 countries across Europe (France, Belgium, Switzerland, Luxembourg, Monaco), North America (Canada, notably Quebec), the Caribbean (Haiti, Martinique, Guadeloupe), Sub-Saharan Africa (Senegal, Ivory Coast, Cameroon, DRC, and many others), and the Pacific (New Caledonia, French Polynesia). The Organisation internationale de la Francophonie counts 88 member states and governments. French is also one of the six official languages of the United Nations, one of three working languages of the European Union alongside English and German, and the primary working language of many international institutions including the International Red Cross, the International Olympic Committee, and Interpol.

### Why It Matters

French matters because it opens doors that few other languages can. A speaker of French can communicate meaningfully across the breadth of African francophone countries — home to some of the fastest-growing economies in the world. In Canada, French is a legal right and a practical necessity in Quebec, New Brunswick, and federal institutions. In Europe, French fluency significantly expands your professional and social reach across five countries and within EU institutions.

Understanding French is valuable because:

- It enables direct participation in French and francophone culture — literature (Molière, Voltaire, Flaubert, Proust, Camus, Simone de Beauvoir), cinema (the French New Wave, contemporary auteur film), cuisine, philosophy, and art
- It develops the analytical mental model for grammatical gender, aspect-based verb systems, and register — directly transferable to learning Spanish, Italian, and other Romance languages substantially faster
- Proficiency is required or highly valued in roles such as diplomat, EU/UN official, NGO worker, luxury goods professional, francophone-market business professional, and academic researcher in history, philosophy, and literature

### Key Features of This Topic

- **CEFR-aligned progression** — Modules follow the internationally recognized Common European Framework of Reference for Languages, from A1 through C1+
- **Pronunciation from day one** — IPA notation and phonetic guidance throughout; French pronunciation is non-intuitive for English speakers and must be addressed early
- **Grammar with meaning** — Grammar rules are always taught with the reasoning behind them, not just the rule itself
- **Authentic language exposure** — From Module 10 onward, the curriculum engages authentic French texts, news, and cultural material rather than simplified learner language
- **DELF/DALF preparation** — Exercises and tests are designed to build toward internationally recognized certification

## Historical Context

French descended from the Vulgar Latin of Roman Gaul through a series of phonological shifts so dramatic that medieval French looks and sounds almost nothing like Classical Latin. The oldest surviving document in recognizable Old French is the **Serments de Strasbourg** (Oaths of Strasbourg), dated **842 CE** — a political oath sworn between two grandsons of Charlemagne, recorded to be intelligible to their respective troops. It is the founding document of French literature.

The language passed through several major phases. **Old French** (9th–13th centuries) retained two grammatical cases (nominative and oblique) and a flexible word order inherited from Latin. **Middle French** (14th–16th centuries) saw the loss of the case system, the rise of a fixed SVO word order, and massive borrowing of learned Latin and Italian vocabulary during the Renaissance. **Classical French** (17th–18th centuries) was the language of the court of Louis XIV at Versailles, of Molière's comedies, Racine's tragedies, and Enlightenment philosophy. It spread across Europe as the prestige language of diplomacy and aristocratic culture — a role it maintained until the mid-20th century.

The **Académie Française**, founded in 1635 by Cardinal Richelieu under Louis XIII, has the mission of standardizing and protecting the French language. It publishes an official dictionary (currently in its 9th edition) and issues guidance on neologisms, foreign borrowings, and usage questions. The Académie's conservatism is a frequent source of public debate: it resisted "courriel" for email, "logiciel" for software, and continues to push back against anglicisms like "le weekend" or "le selfie."

### Verlan and Digital French

Living languages do not stand still. French in the 21st century has been transformed by **Verlan** — a form of argot in which syllables of words are inverted (the word "verlan" is itself "l'envers" — "the reverse" — with syllables swapped). Verlan originated in the banlieues of Paris in the 1970s–80s and entered mainstream slang: "l'envers" → "verlan", "louche" → "chelou", "l'appartement" → "le tepar". Digital French shows heavy anglicization ("checker", "liker", "le scroll"), abbreviation (stp = s'il te plaît, mdr = mort de rire = lol), and emoji-driven pragmatics. C1+ learners must navigate both the formal register of the Académie and the informal register of digital communication.

## Real-World Applications

French is actively used across many domains:

- **International Organizations** — French is a working language at the UN, EU, UNESCO, NATO, the International Court of Justice, and the African Union. Fluent French is frequently a hiring requirement for professional-grade positions in these institutions
- **Business and Trade** — France is the world's 7th largest economy; francophone Africa is a fast-growing investment destination; Quebec is a major economy with French as its legal business language; Belgium and Switzerland are major financial and pharmaceutical hubs
- **Travel** — French speakers have direct linguistic access to France, Belgium, Switzerland, Montreal, New Orleans, francophone Africa, and the French overseas territories. Even basic French dramatically improves travel quality in these regions
- **Literature and Philosophy** — French literature from the 17th century onward is one of the richest in the world. Major philosophical works by Descartes, Sartre, Foucault, Derrida, and Beauvoir were written in French and are best read in the original
- **Cuisine and Hospitality** — The vocabulary of haute cuisine is almost entirely French. Professional cooks, sommeliers, and pastry chefs work with a French technical vocabulary daily
- **Certification (DELF/DALF)** — The **DELF** (Diplôme d'Études en Langue Française, A1–B2) and **DALF** (Diplôme Approfondi de Langue Française, C1–C2) are internationally recognized official certifications issued by the French Ministry of Education. These certificates are required for university admission in France, immigration to francophone countries, and many professional roles

## Learning Objectives

By completing this topic in full, you will be able to:

1. Pronounce French with accuracy sufficient for consistent comprehension by native speakers, including mastery of nasal vowels, liaison, elision, and the uvular r
2. Read and understand a wide range of authentic French texts — news articles, literary fiction, administrative documents, academic writing — with only occasional need for dictionary lookup at C1 level
3. Write formally in French: compose structured essays, formal letters, and reports using varied vocabulary, complex syntax, and appropriate register
4. Speak spontaneously and fluently on familiar and unfamiliar topics, managing conversation structure, turn-taking, and repair strategies
5. Use all major verb tenses and moods correctly: présent, passé composé, imparfait, plus-que-parfait, futur simple, conditionnel présent, subjonctif présent and passé
6. Apply grammatical gender, number agreement, and pronominal substitution accurately across complex sentences
7. Demonstrate cultural literacy in francophone culture: understand references to French history, literature, cinema, and current events in authentic texts
8. Prepare for and pass the DELF B2 examination, and develop the skills needed to sit the DALF C1

## Quick Reference

### Accent Marks

| Accent | Name | Example | Effect |
|--------|------|---------|--------|
| é | accent aigu | *été* (summer) | /e/ — closed e |
| è | accent grave | *père* (father) | /ɛ/ — open e |
| ê | accent circonflexe | *forêt* (forest) | /ɛ/ — often marks historical s |
| ë | tréma | *Noël* (Christmas) | Separate vowel sounds |
| à | accent grave on a | *à* (to/at) | Distinguishes from *a* (has) |
| â | accent circonflexe on a | *pâte* (paste) | /ɑ/ — back a |
| ç | cédille | *garçon* (boy) | /s/ before a, o, u |
| œ | ligature | *sœur* (sister) | /œ/ — open mid-front rounded |

### Essential Pronunciation Rules

| Feature | Rule | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| Final consonants | Usually silent | *Paris* /paʁi/, *grand* /ɡʁɑ̃/ |
| Nasal vowels | Vowel + n/m before consonant = nasal | *pain* /pɛ̃/, *bon* /bɔ̃/ |
| Liaison | Final consonant sounds before vowel-initial word | *les amis* /lez‿ami/ |
| Elision | le/la/je/etc. drop final vowel before vowel | *le + ami* → *l'ami* |
| -er verb endings | Pronounced /e/, not /eʁ/ | *parler* /paʁle/ |
| French r | Uvular fricative, back of throat | *rue* /ʁy/ |

### Key Verb Conjugation Summary (Présent)

| Pronoun | être (to be) | avoir (to have) | aller (to go) | faire (to do/make) |
|---------|-------------|----------------|--------------|-------------------|
| je | suis | ai | vais | fais |
| tu | es | as | vas | fais |
| il/elle | est | a | va | fait |
| nous | sommes | avons | allons | faisons |
| vous | êtes | avez | allez | faites |
| ils/elles | sont | ont | vont | font |
