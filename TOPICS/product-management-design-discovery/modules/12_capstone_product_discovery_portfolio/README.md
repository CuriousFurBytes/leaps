## Overview

This capstone asks you to behave like a product practitioner, not like a student filling in a template. You will choose a realistic opportunity, frame it clearly, gather evidence, design a testable direction, and recommend what the team should do next. The goal is not to produce a perfect answer; the goal is to show disciplined judgment under uncertainty.

A strong portfolio artifact demonstrates how you think. It includes what you learned, what you ruled out, what remains risky, and why your recommendation is proportionate to the evidence. It should be clear enough that an executive, designer, engineer, researcher, and data partner can understand the tradeoff you are asking them to make.

## Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Produce a portfolio-quality discovery case from ambiguous input.
- Justify a product recommendation using evidence, assumptions, risk, and strategy.
- Communicate design and discovery tradeoffs to cross-functional stakeholders.
- Identify what should be built now, tested next, or intentionally not pursued.

## Project Brief

Choose one realistic product opportunity. It may be from your workplace, a public product you use, a nonprofit service, an internal tool, or a plausible startup idea. Do not use confidential data. Your final artifact must include:

```yaml
capstone_packet:
  opportunity: "one-sentence product opportunity"
  audience: "primary customer or user segment"
  evidence_sources:
    - "interviews or observation"
    - "desk research or analytics proxy"
    - "prototype feedback"
  recommendation: "build, test more, pivot, or stop"
  next_30_days: "specific learning or delivery plan"
```

You must let the learner drive. This module provides scaffolding, checkpoints, and hints, but it does not provide a copy-paste solution. If you feel stuck, use the staged help below to decide your next action without skipping the work.

## Milestones

1. **Opportunity framing:** define customer, problem, outcome, constraints, and current alternatives.
2. **Evidence plan:** list assumptions, rank risks, and choose discovery methods that match those risks.
3. **Research synthesis:** collect evidence ethically and convert observations into patterns.
4. **Solution direction:** sketch a concept, service flow, or prototype that addresses the strongest opportunity.
5. **Decision memo:** recommend build, test more, pivot, or stop with confidence level and next step.
6. **Portfolio polish:** make the narrative clear, honest, and reviewable.

## Help and Getting Unstuck

### Hint 1: If the opportunity is too broad

Narrow by segment and situation. "Help managers communicate" is broad; "help first-time engineering managers prepare difficult performance conversations after a missed deadline" is discoverable.

### Hint 2: If evidence conflicts

Separate signal by customer segment, context, and source quality. Conflicting evidence often means you mixed different users or asked questions that measured preference instead of behavior.

### Hint 3: If stakeholders want a roadmap

Show a learning roadmap before a feature roadmap. Use staged bets: highest-risk assumption first, then prototype fidelity, then delivery investment.

```markdown
# Decision Memo Skeleton
## Recommendation
State build, test more, pivot, or stop.
## Evidence
Summarize strongest three signals.
## Risks
Name unresolved assumptions.
## Next Step
Commit to one responsible action.
```

## Acceptance Criteria

- The opportunity is framed as a customer problem and desired outcome, not merely a feature request.
- The evidence plan matches the riskiest assumptions.
- The synthesis distinguishes observations, interpretations, and recommendations.
- The proposed solution is specific enough to critique but not overbuilt.
- The final decision memo states confidence, tradeoffs, and next steps.

## Summary

- The capstone is a realistic product discovery portfolio, not a theory recap.
- Strong product judgment is transparent about assumptions and confidence.
- Discovery evidence should change the recommendation or it was likely performative.
- Design artifacts help teams critique decisions before delivery investment.
- A responsible PM can recommend not building when the evidence does not support it.
