## Why Learn Culinary Patisserie?

Patisserie is the branch of culinary work where flavor, texture, temperature, structure, timing, and visual design meet. A good pastry cook does not merely follow recipes; they understand how flour hydrates, how proteins set, how sugar changes texture, why chocolate crystallizes, and how a production schedule protects quality under real service pressure.

This topic starts from absolute zero: kitchen safety, mise en place, equipment, measuring, and sensory vocabulary. It then moves through doughs, creams, custards, laminated pastry, chocolate, plated desserts, entremets, breads, frozen desserts, production systems, menu design, and professional troubleshooting.

The expert goal is practical judgment. By the end, a learner should be able to design a pastry program, build formulas by weight, test and document changes, cost items, produce consistently, and explain why a product succeeded or failed.

## Prerequisites

- Basic arithmetic: ratios, percentages, unit conversion, and time planning.
- Safe kitchen access with standard home or training-kitchen equipment.
- Willingness to weigh ingredients, document results, and repeat tests carefully.
- Helpful but not required: [[food-safety]], [[chemistry]], and [[business-fundamentals]].

## Quick Reference

| Concept | Practical Rule |
|---|---|
| Weighing | Use grams for repeatability; volume measures hide variation. |
| Mise en place | Read, scale, gather, preheat, prepare tools, then produce. |
| Baker's percentage | Flour or base ingredient is 100%; other ingredients scale against it. |
| Temperature | Track ingredient, room, dough, oven, and storage temperatures. |
| Documentation | Record formula, method, timing, sensory notes, and corrections. |
| Quality control | Judge appearance, aroma, texture, flavor, structure, and consistency. |

## How to Use This Topic

Work through the modules in order. For each module, read the theory, complete the exercises, attempt the test without the answer key, and log production observations in `NOTES.md`. Treat every recipe as an experiment: change one variable at a time and write down what happened.
