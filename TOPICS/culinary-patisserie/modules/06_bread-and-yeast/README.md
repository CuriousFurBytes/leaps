## Overview

Bread is the most ancient of human cooking achievements and one of the most scientifically
complex. A simple loaf of bread requires the precise management of fermentation (living yeast
organisms consuming sugars and producing carbon dioxide), gluten development (protein networks
giving structure), moisture (hydration determining crumb texture), and heat (the transformation
from wet dough to crusty loaf in a matter of minutes in a hot oven).

Understanding bread from a scientific perspective transforms it from a mysterious, unpredictable
process into a manageable, reproducible craft. The baker's percentage system allows you to scale
any recipe and compare formulas directly. The windowpane test gives you a physical test for
gluten development that does not depend on time. And understanding sourdough fermentation opens
the door to the most complex, flavourful breads in the world.

## Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

1. Explain baker's percentages and convert any recipe to baker's percentage notation
2. Describe the role of yeast in bread fermentation: what yeast consumes, what it produces, and why temperature affects fermentation rate
3. Develop gluten correctly through kneading, and perform the windowpane test to assess development
4. Bake a basic white sandwich loaf from a commercial yeast recipe with correct texture and crust
5. Maintain a sourdough starter culture through regular feeding cycles for at least one week
6. Understand the role of hydration (the baker's percentage of water) in determining crumb structure
7. Bake a baguette or similar high-hydration lean dough with an open crumb and crisp crust

## Theory

> **STUB** — Full theory to be written. Topics to cover:
>
> - Baker's percentages: flour is always 100%; all other ingredients expressed relative to flour weight
> - Yeast biology: *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* in commercial yeast; wild yeast in sourdough; alcoholic fermentation: glucose → ethanol + CO₂; why this matters for rise and flavour
> - Gluten development: glutenin + gliadin proteins combine with water under mechanical stress (kneading) to form gluten networks; these networks trap CO₂ bubbles and give bread its structure
> - The windowpane test: stretch a small piece of dough; if it stretches thin enough to be translucent without tearing, gluten is fully developed
> - The stages of bread making: mixing → bulk fermentation (first rise) → shaping → final proof → bake
> - Autolyse: resting flour and water before adding other ingredients; promotes gluten development with less kneading
> - Hydration levels and their effect: 60% hydration → soft crumb, easy to handle; 75%+ → open, irregular crumb, sticky dough
> - Sourdough starter: a culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria; feeding cycles; when a starter is "ripe" and ready to use
> - Steam in the oven: the first 10–15 minutes of baking require steam to keep the crust extensible; allows maximum rise before crust sets
> - Maillard reaction on the crust: final crust colour and flavour from browning reactions between proteins and sugars at 140–180 °C
> - Staling: retrogradation of starch crystallises and firms the crumb; bread goes stale faster at refrigerator temperatures than at room temperature

## Key Concepts

- **Baker's percentage** — a ratio system where flour is always 100% and all other ingredients are expressed as a percentage of the flour weight; see [[culinary-patisserie#cheatsheet]]
- **Gluten** — the protein network formed by glutenin and gliadin in wheat flour when mixed with water and subjected to mechanical force; provides structure and elasticity
- **Fermentation** — the conversion of sugars to CO₂ and ethanol by yeast; the source of rise, flavour complexity, and keeping quality in bread
- **Windowpane test** — a physical test for gluten development: stretch a small piece of dough thin enough to see light through without tearing
- **Sourdough** — bread leavened by a starter culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria; characterised by acidity (from lactic and acetic acids), complex flavour, and longer fermentation

## Common Pitfalls

> **STUB** — Common pitfalls to be documented:
>
> - Over-proofing (yeast exhausts sugars; dough collapses; dense crumb)
> - Under-kneading (insufficient gluten; loaf tears and spreads sideways)
> - Baking without steam (thick, rigid crust forms too early; limits rise; "oven spring" is suppressed)
> - Killing yeast with hot water (water above 45 °C kills commercial yeast)
> - Sourdough starter not fed regularly (bacteria overproduce acid; yeast weakens; poor rise)

## Summary

> **STUB** — To be written on full module completion.

- Baker's percentages allow any recipe to be scaled or compared; flour is always 100%
- Yeast ferments sugars to CO₂ (for rise) and ethanol (for flavour); temperature and time control the rate
- Gluten is the structural network in wheat dough; the windowpane test measures its development
- Hydration determines crumb texture: low hydration = dense soft crumb; high hydration = open irregular crumb
- Sourdough introduces wild yeast and bacteria; longer fermentation builds complex flavour and acidity
