## Overview

A plated dessert is a complete sensory experience: flavour, texture, temperature contrast, and
visual composition all working together on a single plate. This module combines the technical
production of three classical desserts — panna cotta (gelatin-set cream), crème brûlée
(baked custard with caramelised sugar crust), and chocolate mousse (aerated ganache stabilised
by egg foam) — with the visual language of professional plating.

Plating is not decoration for its own sake. Every element on a plate — every dot of sauce,
every crisp tuile, every quenelled ice cream — serves both flavour and composition. Understanding
the principles of visual composition (the rule of thirds, contrast of colour and texture, the
deliberate use of negative space) gives you a vocabulary for designing plates rather than simply
arranging food.

## Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

1. Make a panna cotta with correct gelatin ratio (set but trembling; not rubbery)
2. Make a crème brûlée with silky custard texture and a perfectly caramelised sugar crust using a blowtorch
3. Make a chocolate mousse using the classic method (ganache base + Italian meringue or whipped cream)
4. Execute a sauce dot, a sauce swipe, and a quenelle correctly on a cold plate
5. Plate a dessert applying the rule of thirds, height variation, and texture contrast
6. Explain the role of each element on a composed dessert plate (anchor, sauce, garnish, crumb)

## Theory

> **STUB** — Full theory to be written. Topics to cover:
>
> **Desserts:**
> - Panna cotta: gelatin-set cream; gelatin hydration and blooming; the ratio of gelatin to liquid determines set firmness; standard is 7–10 g gelatin per 500 ml cream for a trembling set
> - Crème brûlée: a baked egg custard (covered in Module 05) + the caramelisation of the sugar crust; blowtorch technique vs. grill/broiler; the sugar crust must be thin and glassy
> - Chocolate mousse: two methods — (1) ganache base folded with whipped cream (simpler, rich); (2) ganache base + Italian meringue (lighter, more stable); both use aeration for texture
> - Italian meringue: hot sugar syrup cooked to 118 °C poured onto whipping egg whites — produces a stable, glossy meringue that does not weep; used in mousses, buttercreams, and soufflés
>
> **Plating Composition:**
> - The plate as a canvas: negative space is not wasted space — it directs the eye
> - The rule of thirds: divide the plate into a 3×3 grid; place the main element at an intersection, not the centre
> - Texture contrast: every successful plate has a contrast of textures (crisp vs. soft, smooth vs. rough) — the toffee shard on a panna cotta; the tuile on a mousse
> - Temperature contrast: hot sauce + cold ice cream; warm chocolate fondant + cold crème anglaise
> - Colour: natural flavour pairings often produce good colour contrast (dark chocolate + bright orange sauce; pale panna cotta + deep berry coulis)
> - Elements of a composed plate: anchor (main element), sauce, garnish, crumb/crisp element
> - The quenelle: a three-sided oval shape formed by passing between two spoons; used for ice cream, sorbet, mousse, and cream; requires a cold, slightly fluid base
> - Sauce dots and swipes: use a squeeze bottle for dots; a small offset palette knife or spoon for swipes; work on a cold, dry plate

## Key Concepts

- **Panna cotta** — gelatin-set cream dessert; the Italian equivalent of a bavarois; set texture determined by gelatin ratio
- **Gelatin** — a protein derived from collagen; sets liquids to a gel when cooled; must be bloomed in cold water before use; loses setting power if boiled
- **Italian meringue** — egg whites stabilised by cooked sugar syrup; more stable than French or Swiss meringue; used in mousses and buttercreams
- **Quenelle** — a three-sided oval shape formed between two spoons; the standard elegant presentation for ice cream, mousse, and cream
- **Sauce dot** — a precise small circle of sauce applied with a squeeze bottle; spacing and size deliberate

## Common Pitfalls

> **STUB** — Common pitfalls to be documented:
>
> - Too much gelatin in panna cotta (rubbery, bounces on the plate — not silky)
> - Sugar crust too thick on crème brûlée (won't caramelise evenly; thick layer absorbs heat before liquefying)
> - Mousse deflated by over-folding (knocks out the trapped air)
> - Plating on warm plates (sauce runs; gelatin-set desserts begin to melt immediately)

## Summary

> **STUB** — To be written on full module completion.

- Panna cotta requires precise gelatin ratios; too little and it won't set; too much and it becomes rubbery
- Chocolate mousse is aerated ganache; the method of aeration (whipped cream vs. meringue) determines texture and stability
- The crème brûlée sugar crust must be thin (1–2 mm) and caramelised rapidly with a blowtorch
- Every plated dessert has anchor, sauce, garnish, and crumb/crisp elements — each serves both flavour and visual purpose
- The quenelle and sauce dot are the two most important plating techniques for desserts
