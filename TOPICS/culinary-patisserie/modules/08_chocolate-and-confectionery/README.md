## Overview

Chocolate is the most technically demanding confectionery ingredient, and the reason lies in its
fat. Cocoa butter — the fat naturally present in cacao — is polymorphic: it can solidify into
six distinct crystal structures (Form I through Form VI), each with a different melting point
and texture. Only Form V produces the glossy, snapping, palate-melting chocolate that a
professional confectioner expects. Tempering is the process of guiding cocoa butter into
Form V crystals through a precise sequence of heating, cooling, and reheating.

This module also covers ganache — the emulsion of cream and chocolate that is the foundation
of truffles, bonbon fillings, glazes, and chocolate tart fillings — and the hands-on techniques
of hand-rolling truffles, piping centres, and coating in tempered chocolate.

## Theory

> **STUB** — Full theory to be written. Topics to cover:
>
> - Cacao to chocolate: the agricultural and industrial process
>   - Cacao pod: fermentation of cacao beans (develops flavour precursors)
>   - Drying, roasting (further Maillard development of chocolate flavour)
>   - Winnowing (separating nibs from shells), grinding (melting nibs to cacao mass)
>   - Adding sugar, milk (for milk chocolate), lecithin (emulsifier)
>   - Conching: extended agitation at elevated temperature; develops smooth texture and removes volatile acids
>   - Commercial tempering before pouring into moulds
>
> - Cocoa butter polymorphism: six crystal forms (I–VI)
>   - Forms I–IV: unstable; low melting points; dull, soft, grainy texture
>   - Form V: stable at room temperature (mp ~34 °C); glossy; hard snap; melts smoothly at body temperature — the target
>   - Form VI: over-stable; high melting point; waxy texture; develops in very old chocolate
>   - Why tempering matters: untempered chocolate has a mix of crystal forms; results in "bloom" (grey streaks from fat migration), soft texture, and poor snap
>
> - The tempering process (tabling method):
>   - Melt chocolate to 50–55 °C (dark), destroying all existing crystals
>   - Pour two-thirds onto a cold marble surface; work with a palette knife and scraper
>   - Cool to 27–28 °C (for dark), seeding Form V crystal formation in the cooled portion
>   - Return to main mass; reheat gently to 31–32 °C (dark); this melts unstable forms (I–IV) while preserving Form V
>   - Test: smear a thin layer on the marble; it should set within 5 minutes with a glossy, matte-free surface
>
> - See [[culinary-patisserie#glossary]] for *tempérer* definition
>
> - Ganache: an emulsion of cream and chocolate
>   - Cream provides water and fat; chocolate provides fat, cocoa particles, and sugar
>   - Lecithin in chocolate (from soy or sunflower) aids emulsification
>   - Ratios and their results: 1:1 (sauce, glaze), 1:2 (soft truffle centre), 1:3 (firm, sliceable)
>   - Technique: heat cream to 80 °C; pour over chopped chocolate; stir from centre outward to build emulsion
>   - Flavour infusions: herbs, spices, citrus zest steeped in cream before straining
>
> - Truffles: ganache cooled, portioned, hand-rolled into spheres, coated
> - Moulded bonbons: mould pre-coated with tempered chocolate, filled with flavoured ganache,
>   capped with second layer of tempered chocolate; demoulded when set

## Key Concepts

- **Tempering (tempérer)** — the controlled temperature sequence that guides cocoa butter into Form V crystals; see [[culinary-patisserie#glossary]]
- **Form V crystals** — the target crystal polymorph: stable, glossy, snapping, melts at body temperature
- **Bloom** — the grey, streaky surface on untempered or improperly stored chocolate; fat bloom (cocoa butter migration) or sugar bloom (moisture contact)
- **Ganache** — an emulsion of cream and chocolate; see [[culinary-patisserie#glossary]]
- **Tabling method** — hand-tempering technique using a marble surface to cool and seed chocolate

## Common Pitfalls

> **STUB** — Common pitfalls to be documented:
>
> - Seizing: adding water to chocolate (water makes chocolate seize into a grainy paste; all equipment must be dry)
> - Ganache breaks (emulsion fails): usually due to incorrect temperature ratio; rescue by blending gently
> - Bloom: under-tempered (Form V not achieved) or temperature fluctuation during storage
> - Chocolate too cool when moulding (sets too fast; incomplete coverage; air pockets)

## Summary

> **STUB** — To be written on full module completion.

- Cocoa butter is polymorphic; Form V is the target crystal that produces glossy, snapping, palate-melting chocolate
- Tempering is a precise temperature sequence: melt (50–55 °C), cool (27–28 °C), reheat (31–32 °C) for dark chocolate
- Ganache is an emulsion; the ratio of cream to chocolate determines texture from sauce to firm slice
- The tabling method is the hand-tempering technique: marble surface, palette knife, thermometer
- Chocolate must be kept completely dry; a drop of water causes seizing — the irreversible grainy collapse of the emulsion
