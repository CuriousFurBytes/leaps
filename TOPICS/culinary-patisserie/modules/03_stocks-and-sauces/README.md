## Overview

No professional kitchen in the classical French tradition operates without stocks. A stock
(*fond* in French — literally "base" or "foundation") is the extracted essence of bones,
shells, or vegetables simmered in water with aromatics. Stocks are the liquid medium in which
sauces develop their depth, braises build their complexity, and risottos absorb their flavour.

The five **mother sauces** (*sauces mères*) codified by Escoffier are the conceptual backbone
of classical French sauce-making. Each is a distinct preparation method, and each spawns a
family of derivative sauces. Mastering the five mother sauces means mastering the underlying
principles — the thickening agents, the heat management, the seasoning logic — that apply to
every sauce in classical and modern cooking.

## Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

1. Prepare a white chicken stock from scratch, applying correct technique (cold water start, no boil, skimming)
2. Prepare a brown veal or beef stock using the roasting method for deep colour and flavour
3. Prepare a fish stock (*fumet*) and a vegetable stock
4. Make all five mother sauces: Béchamel, Velouté, Espagnole, Hollandaise, Sauce Tomat
5. Derive at least one secondary sauce from each mother sauce
6. Explain the role of *fond*, *roux*, *liaison*, and *nappe* in sauce-making
7. Diagnose and correct a broken Hollandaise

## Theory

> **STUB** — Full theory to be written. Topics to cover:
>
> - What makes a stock: gelatin extraction from collagen, the flavour compounds in bones,
>   the role of mirepoix and bouquet garni
> - White stock: cold water, gentle simmer, skimming, never boil (cloudiness from fat emulsification)
> - Brown stock: roasting bones and mirepoix for the Maillard reaction; deglazing the roasting pan
> - Fish stock (fumet): much shorter cooking time (20–30 min max); over-cooking extracts bitter compounds
> - Vegetable stock: balance of sweet (carrot), savoury (celery), aromatic (leek); avoiding starchy veg
> - The five mother sauces:
>   - Béchamel (roux + milk): the white sauce foundation; derivatives include Mornay, Soubise
>   - Velouté (roux + white stock): foundation for fish or chicken sauces; derivatives include Suprême, Allemande
>   - Espagnole (brown roux + brown stock + tomato paste): foundation for demi-glace and all brown sauces
>   - Hollandaise (egg yolk + clarified butter emulsion): the "warm emulsion" mother sauce
>   - Sauce Tomat (tomato + stock): the simplest mother sauce
> - Thickening agents: roux, beurre manié, liaison (egg yolk + cream), reduction, starch slurries
> - The nappe test and how to read sauce consistency

## Key Concepts

- **Fond** — caramelised browned bits in a pan; the concentrated Maillard flavour base; see [[culinary-patisserie#glossary]]
- **Roux** — cooked equal-weight mixture of flour and butter; thickening base for béchamel and velouté
- **Nappe** — the consistency at which a sauce coats and holds on the back of a spoon; target for crème anglaise and many sauces
- **Liaison** — a thickening or binding agent; the term covers roux, egg yolk, cream, and starch slurries
- **Beurre blanc** — emulsified butter sauce from white wine and shallot reduction; see [[culinary-patisserie#glossary]]
- **Demi-glace** — reduced espagnole mixed with equal parts brown stock; the intensely flavoured foundation for classical brown sauces

## Common Pitfalls

> **STUB** — Common pitfalls to be documented:
>
> - Boiling the stock (causes cloudy, bitter result)
> - Not skimming the stock in the first 30 minutes (grey scum from blood and proteins)
> - Over-cooking fish stock (bitter; 20–30 min maximum)
> - Hollandaise too hot (scrambles the egg yolk)
> - Hollandaise too cold (won't emulsify)
> - Adding cold butter to hollandaise instead of warm clarified butter

## Summary

> **STUB** — To be written on full module completion.

- Stocks are cold-water extractions of gelatin and flavour from bones, aromatics, and mirepoix
- Never boil a stock — a gentle simmer extracts clarity and gelatin without cloudiness
- The five mother sauces are the conceptual framework for all classical French saucing
- Each mother sauce has a distinct thickening mechanism and spawns a family of derivatives
- Hollandaise is the most technically demanding — an emulsion that requires precise temperature control
