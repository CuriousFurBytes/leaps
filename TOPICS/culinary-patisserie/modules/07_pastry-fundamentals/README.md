## Overview

Pastry doughs are the architecture of patisserie. Where bread-making seeks to maximise gluten
development to trap fermentation gases, pastry-making seeks the opposite: to minimise gluten
formation while creating specific textures through careful management of fat and water.

*Pâte brisée* (short-crust pastry) is the all-purpose savoury pastry shell: flaky, buttery,
neutral in flavour. *Pâte sucrée* (sweet pastry) is richer and more tender, with added sugar
that inhibits gluten and creates a crisp, biscuit-like texture ideal for tart shells. *Pâte
feuilletée* (puff pastry) is the most technically demanding of the three — a laminated dough
in which hundreds of alternating layers of dough and butter are created through repeated folding
and rolling, producing the characteristic lift and flakiness through steam expansion during baking.

## Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

1. Explain why pastry doughs minimise gluten development, and describe three techniques used to achieve this
2. Make pâte brisée using the rubbing method (*sablage*), rest, roll, and blind-bake a tart shell without shrinkage
3. Make pâte sucrée using the *crémage* method, and explain how sugar and fat ratios differ from brisée
4. Make a rough puff pastry (*demi-feuilletée*) with at least 100 visible layers
5. Explain the science of lamination: how alternating fat and dough layers create steam-lifted separation during baking
6. Complete a simple fruit tart (pâte sucrée + pastry cream + fresh fruit + glaze)
7. Identify and explain the visual signs of over-worked vs. correctly made short pastry

## Theory

> **STUB** — Full theory to be written. Topics to cover:
>
> - The three enemies of short pastry: over-working, warmth, and excess water — all promote gluten
> - The rubbing method (*sablage*): rubbing cold butter into flour to create a mealy, sand-like texture; fat coats flour particles before water is added, physically limiting gluten formation
> - The crémage method (for pâte sucrée): creaming butter and sugar first; the sugar draws water away from flour; results in more tender, crumbly texture
> - Resting dough: why dough must rest in the fridge — gluten relaxes, fat firms, water distributes evenly
> - Blind baking: baking a pastry shell with weights (dried beans or ceramic weights) to prevent the base from puffing and the sides from collapsing
> - Laminated dough (*feuilletage*): the *détrempe* (base dough) and *beurrage* (butter block) are combined; through repeated folding (*tourage*), layers multiply; 6 folds × 3 layers per fold = 729 layers of dough/butter at minimum
> - Steam expansion: each butter layer contains water (about 16%); when the oven is hot, this water flashes to steam, pushing layers apart to create the classic lift and flakiness
> - The role of cold temperature in lamination: butter must remain plastic (not liquid) during the folding process; working on a chilled surface is essential
> - Pastry cream (*crème pâtissière*): a thick custard made with cornflour as primary thickener; the standard filling for fruit tarts and éclairs
> - Common tarts: tarte aux pommes, tarte au citron, tarte tatin (caramelised apple upside-down tart)
> - *Feuilletage* — see [[culinary-patisserie#glossary]]

## Key Concepts

- **Pâte brisée** — short-crust pastry; rubbing method; neutral flavour; used for quiche and savoury tarts
- **Pâte sucrée** — sweet short pastry; crémage method; sugar + fat inhibit gluten; crisp biscuit texture
- **Pâte feuilletée** — laminated puff pastry; hundreds of alternating dough and butter layers; steam lifts layers during baking
- **Feuilletage** — the laminated structure of puff pastry; see [[culinary-patisserie#glossary]]
- **Blind baking** — pre-baking a pastry shell with weights before filling; prevents puffing and shrinkage
- **Crème pâtissière** — thick cornflour-set custard cream; the standard pastry filling

## Common Pitfalls

> **STUB** — Common pitfalls to be documented:
>
> - Warm butter (melts into flour rather than coating it; creates mealy, greasy pastry)
> - Over-working the dough (develops gluten; pastry shrinks during baking; tough texture)
> - Not resting the dough (gluten not relaxed; shrinkage during baking)
> - Laminated dough too warm during folding (butter melts into dough; layers merge; no lift)

## Summary

> **STUB** — To be written on full module completion.

- Short pastry (brisée and sucrée) minimises gluten by coating flour in fat before adding water
- Cold temperature is essential throughout: warm butter destroys the fat-coating mechanism
- Puff pastry creates hundreds of alternating dough/butter layers that separate into flaky sheets through steam
- Blind baking sets the pastry shell before filling; without it, liquid fillings produce soggy pastry
- Rest time is not optional: gluten must relax between kneading and baking to prevent shrinkage
