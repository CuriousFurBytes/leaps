## Overview

A chef who can cook brilliantly but cannot cost a menu is not ready to run a kitchen professionally.
Food cost management is the economic foundation of every restaurant, catering operation, and food
business. Understanding food cost percentage, yield loss, and menu engineering transforms cooking
skill into viable professional practice.

This module bridges the craft of cooking with the business of food. It covers how to calculate
the true cost of a dish (accounting for trim loss and yield), how to set a target food cost
percentage, how to analyse a menu for profitability and popularity, and how to write a standard
recipe card — the document that allows any trained cook to reproduce your dish with consistent
quality.

Nutritional balance is also covered: a menu is not only a financial document but a nutritional
one. Understanding macronutrient balance, portion sizing, and dietary restriction management
is essential for professional and home menu planning.

## Theory

> **STUB** — Full theory to be written. Topics to cover:
>
> **Food Cost Percentage:**
> - Formula: food cost % = (cost of ingredients / selling price) × 100
> - Industry benchmarks: fine dining: 28–35%; casual: 25–30%; catering: 20–28%
> - Why food cost percentage is not the only metric: labour, overhead, and portion consistency all affect profitability
>
> **Yield Calculation:**
> - Yield percentage = (usable weight / as-purchased weight) × 100
> - Example: a 1 kg bunch of asparagus may yield 700 g after trimming (70% yield)
> - Cost per usable portion = (price per kg / yield %) × portion weight
> - Why yield matters: a fillet of salmon appears cheaper than whole salmon per kg, but yield calculation often reverses the economics
>
> **Standard Recipe Card:**
> - Components: dish name, yield, portions, ingredients (weight/volume), procedure, plating instructions, allergens, photo
> - Why it matters: ensures consistency regardless of which cook executes the dish; the basis for food cost calculation
>
> **Menu Engineering:**
> - The BCG matrix applied to menus: two axes — contribution margin (profitability) and popularity (sales volume)
> - Stars: high margin, high volume — feature and promote
> - Plough Horses: low margin, high volume — reduce cost or raise price
> - Puzzles: high margin, low volume — better positioning/description
> - Dogs: low margin, low volume — remove or redesign
>
> **Nutritional Balance in a Menu:**
> - Macronutrient progression across courses: the classic tasting menu moves from light (amuse-bouche, entrée) to rich (main) to sweet (dessert)
> - Avoiding flavour fatigue: vary textures, temperatures, and flavour profiles across courses
> - Dietary restrictions as design constraints: allergen management (nuts, dairy, gluten, shellfish); vegetarian/vegan substitutions; religious dietary laws
> - Portion sizing: tasting menu portions are intentionally small (3–4 bites per course); the pleasure is cumulative, not volumetric

## Key Concepts

- **Food cost percentage** — the ratio of ingredient cost to selling price; the primary financial metric in food service
- **Yield percentage** — the proportion of an as-purchased ingredient that is usable after trimming or processing
- **Standard recipe card** — the written specification of a dish enabling consistent, costed reproduction
- **Menu engineering** — the analysis of a menu by profitability and popularity to guide pricing and repositioning decisions
- **Contribution margin** — the profit contribution per dish (selling price minus food cost); higher is better

## Common Pitfalls

> **STUB** — Common pitfalls to be documented:
>
> - Calculating food cost without accounting for yield (underestimates true cost)
> - Setting price to achieve food cost target without considering portion satisfaction
> - Recipe cards without allergen information (professional and legal risk)
> - Menu fatigue: multiple rich, heavy dishes without textural or flavour contrast
