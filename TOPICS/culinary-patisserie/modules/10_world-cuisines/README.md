## Overview

Every cuisine in the world is a response to the same fundamental challenge: how do we transform
the available ingredients of a particular place and time into nourishing, pleasurable food? The
French classical tradition has been the primary lens for this topic, but it is one tradition
among hundreds. Understanding cuisine comparatively — seeing what different traditions share and
where they diverge — produces a more flexible and genuinely literate cook.

This module surveys three traditions chosen for their depth, their contrast, and their direct
relevance to the learner:

**Brazilian regional cuisine** is one of the most diverse national cuisines in the world,
reflecting Indigenous, Portuguese colonial, African (brought by the slave trade), and immigrant
(Japanese, Italian, German, Lebanese) influences. Three major regional traditions are profiled:
*nordestina* (the northeast: sun-dried beef, manioc flour, coconut milk, dendê oil), *mineira*
(Minas Gerais: beans and rice, pork fat, tutu de feijão, pão de queijo), and *paulistana*
(São Paulo: urban diversity, immigrant cuisines, virado à paulista).

**Italian culinary fundamentals** provide a counterpoint to French cuisine: while French cooking
emphasises complex sauces and preparation techniques, Italian cooking emphasises ingredient
quality, simplicity, and regional specificity. Pasta technique, risotto method, and the
principle of *cucina povera* (peasant cooking) are the focus.

**Japanese knife skills and sashimi** represent a tradition in which the knife is treated as a
precision instrument of the highest order, and the chef's respect for the raw ingredient is the
primary culinary value. *Shokunin* (artisan) culture, the *yanagiba* knife, and the correct
cutting angles for sashimi slices are covered.

## Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

1. Describe the three major Brazilian regional cuisine traditions and name three characteristic dishes and ingredients from each
2. Cook a simple *tutu de feijão* (bean purée with bacon and manioc flour) applying the technique correctly
3. Cook a risotto *all'onda* ("wave" consistency — loose, flowing, not stiff) using the correct absorption method
4. Explain the Japanese *shokunin* philosophy and its influence on knife use and ingredient respect
5. Demonstrate the correct angle and stroke for sashimi cuts using a *yanagiba* (or equivalent long slicer)
6. Explain why sashimi-grade fish requires different handling from cooked fish, and describe the food safety requirements
7. Compare and contrast French classical, Italian, and Japanese culinary values on three dimensions: technique complexity, ingredient focus, and sauce philosophy

## Theory

> **STUB** — Full theory to be written. Topics to cover:
>
> **Brazilian Regional Cuisine:**
> - Introduction: the geography and history of Brazilian food; Indigenous staples (manioc/cassava, açaí, guaraná), Portuguese influence (bacalhau, rice and beans), African influence (dendê oil, vatapá, acarajé), immigrant cuisines (Japanese community in São Paulo)
> - Nordestina: the northeast sertão; *carne de sol* (sun-dried/cured beef); *moqueca baiana* (fish stew with coconut milk and dendê oil); *baião de dois* (rice and beans with coalho cheese); *rapadura*
> - Mineira: Minas Gerais; *feijão tropeiro* (cowboy beans); *tutu de feijão*; *pão de queijo*; the role of *banha de porco* (pork fat); dairy tradition of Minas
> - Paulistana: São Paulo as a melting pot; *virado à paulista*; the Japanese community and sushi culture; *coxinha*; the Italian heritage in pasta and pizza
>
> **Italian Fundamentals:**
> - *Cucina povera*: the tradition of making extraordinary food from simple, humble ingredients; the antithesis of French elaborate technique
> - Pasta: fresh vs. dry; regional traditions (Bologna → tagliatelle; Rome → cacio e pepe; Naples → spaghetti); cooking *al dente*; pasta water as a sauce component (starch emulsifies)
> - Risotto: Arborio/Carnaroli rice; the absorption method (add stock gradually, stir constantly); *all'onda* consistency; *mantecatura* (beating in cold butter at the end for creaminess)
> - Soffritto: Italian mirepoix (onion, celery, carrot); the foundation of countless Italian braises and sauces
> - *Mise en place* in Italian cooking: less rigidly formalised than French, but the principle is identical
>
> **Japanese Knife Skills and Sashimi:**
> - The *shokunin* philosophy: a craftsperson who devotes their life to one discipline; the sushi or sashimi chef as artisan, not just cook
> - The *yanagiba*: the long, single-bevel, thin sashimi knife; cutting on the push stroke with a single drawing motion (not sawing)
> - Fish anatomy for sashimi: working with the grain of the muscle fibre; why cut angle matters for texture
> - *Saku* blocks: preparing a block of sashimi-grade fish for slicing
> - Food safety for raw fish: what "sashimi grade" means; parasite risk (anisakis in salmon and certain white fish); freezing requirements (-20 °C for 7 days kills parasites)
> - *Hirazukuri* (flat slice), *sogizukuri* (diagonal slice), *usuzukuri* (paper-thin slice): the three principal sashimi cuts

## Key Concepts

- **Nordestina cuisine** — the culinary tradition of northeast Brazil; defined by sun-dried beef, manioc flour, coconut milk, and dendê oil
- **Mineira cuisine** — the food culture of Minas Gerais; beans, pork fat, dairy; home cooking elevated
- **Cucina povera** — Italian peasant cooking philosophy: extraordinary results from humble ingredients; the opposite of French elaboration
- **Soffritto** — Italian aromatic base: onion, celery, carrot sautéed in olive oil (the Italian counterpart to mirepoix)
- **Risotto all'onda** — correctly cooked risotto at "wave" consistency: loose, flowing, not stiff; achieved through gradual stock addition and *mantecatura*
- **Shokunin** — the Japanese artisan philosophy; applied to culinary work, it represents a lifetime of dedicated craft refinement
- **Yanagiba** — the Japanese long, single-bevel sashimi knife; cuts on the pull stroke without sawing

## Common Pitfalls

> **STUB** — Common pitfalls to be documented:
>
> - Risotto stirred too violently or too rarely (correct: constant gentle movement)
> - Risotto overcooked — stiff, dry, not *all'onda* (add more warm stock; never add cold)
> - Sashimi sliced against the grain (wrong texture; should follow muscle fibre direction)
> - Using non-sashimi-grade fish for raw preparations
